What a VIN Is Called Around the World

Chassis number, frame number, FIN - the same idea, a different name in every country.

Ask someone in the United States for a car's VIN and they know exactly what you mean. Ask the same question in the UK, India, or the Philippines and you are far more likely to hear "chassis number." In Germany the paperwork says FIN. In Japan the document reads 車台番号. It is often the same identifier, just under a name that fits local habit and local law. This page lists what the number is called region by region, so you know which word to use and what you are actually looking at. To read a full number automatically, use our VIN Decoder.

One Global Standard, Many Local Names

The modern VIN is defined by two international standards. ISO 3779 sets the 17-character format and what each part means, and ISO 3780 defines the World Manufacturer Identifier at the start of it. Most countries that build or import cars have adopted this 17-character number. What changes from place to place is the word people use for it and, in a few countries, whether the local system relies on the full VIN at all.

The 17-character VIN did not appear everywhere at once. The United States made it mandatory for the 1981 model year. Australia required it from 1 January 1989. Older cars in many markets carry a shorter chassis number that predates the standard, with no check digit and no encoded year. So "chassis number" can mean the modern VIN or an older, shorter stamp, depending on the car's age and where it was built.

What a VIN Is Called by Country

Here is the term you will actually see on documents and hear from sellers in each market, with notes on how the local system treats it. Every entry below was checked against local sources.

Chassis Number vs Engine Number vs VIN

Outside North America the biggest source of confusion is not the VIN itself but the engine number sitting next to it. They are two different things:

  • The VIN (or chassis number) identifies the whole vehicle. It stays with the car for life and is the number you use to check history, ownership, and recalls.
  • The engine number identifies only the engine. It has no fixed length or format, its location varies by maker, and it changes if the engine is ever replaced.

In countries like India and Pakistan both numbers appear on the registration document, which is why buyers there often quote them together. For more on that split, see our guide on the difference between a VIN and an engine number.

Where the 17-Character VIN Is and Isn't Used

The 17-character standard is close to universal for cars built from the 1980s onward, but there are real exceptions worth knowing before you try to decode a number:

  • Older cars. Anything built before the standard reached that market uses a shorter chassis number. See our guide to decoding a pre-1981 VIN.
  • Japanese domestic cars. A car sold new in Japan and never exported often has only a chassis/frame number, not a 17-character VIN. The same car built for export does get one.
  • Motorcycles. Bikes use a frame number, which on modern models is the VIN but on older or domestic-market bikes may be a shorter maker-specific code.
  • Locally built vehicles. Some vehicles made for a single home market, such as certain Philippine jeepneys, carry a chassis number without a full ISO VIN.

A Note on Our Tools and Non-US VINs

Our decoder and validator are built around the North American standard, which follows the ISO format and includes the mandatory check digit in position 9. That works cleanly for VINs from the US, Canada, and Mexico, and for most modern export vehicles.

A VIN from Europe or Asia may still decode for country and manufacturer, but it can fail the check-digit test, because not every market enforces that digit the way North America does. That is expected behaviour, not a bug in the number. How to Find the Number, Wherever You Are

The name changes but the location rarely does. On most modern cars the VIN or chassis number is visible at the base of the windscreen (the side varies, and on right-hand-drive cars it is often the passenger side), on the driver's door jamb, and printed on the registration document.
