Buying a used car from a stranger comes with one question you cannot skip: is it stolen? The VIN is your best tool for answering it, but not in the way most people think. The number itself never says "stolen." What it does is unlock the databases that do. Here is how to run the check, which free services to use, and the signs that should stop you cold.

## The VIN Is the Key, Not the Answer

A VIN is a 17-character fingerprint. On its own, it tells you the maker, the country, the model year, and the plant. It does not carry a theft flag. To learn whether a car is stolen, you take the VIN and look it up in databases that track stolen and salvaged vehicles.

So the process is two steps. First, make sure the VIN is genuine and matches the car. Then, run that VIN through the right services.

## Step 1: Confirm the VIN Is Real and Matches

Before you trust any lookup, confirm the number itself. A cloned or altered VIN can return a clean result because it is copying an honest car.

- Read the VIN off the dashboard and the driver's door jamb. They must match.
- Check it against the title, character for character.
- Validate the format with our [VIN Validator](/vin-validator/), then decode it with the [VIN Decoder](/vin-decoder/) and confirm the maker and year fit the actual car.

If the plates disagree or the number does not describe the car in front of you, stop. That alone is a reason to walk away, and our guide on [how VIN cloning scams work](/blog/how-vin-cloning-scams-work/) explains why.

## Step 2: Search the Right Databases

Once you trust the number, look it up. Several services check stolen and title records:

| Service | Region | What it checks |
|---|---|---|
| NICB VINCheck | United States | Insurance theft and salvage records, free |
| NMVTIS | United States | Title, brand, and salvage history |
| Local police / DMV | Most countries | Stolen vehicle reports |
| Paid history reports | Global | Accidents, ownership, title, theft flags |

In the United States, the National Insurance Crime Bureau runs a free VINCheck tool that flags cars reported stolen and not recovered, plus salvage records from member insurers. Outside the US, your national vehicle agency or police service usually offers a stolen-vehicle check. For a full picture, a paid history report pulls theft, title, and accident data into one place.

## Step 3: Ask the Police for Anything Expensive

Free databases are a strong first pass, but they do not catch everything. For a costly purchase, take the VIN to your local police and ask them to run it. Many departments will check a number against live stolen records on request. It costs nothing and closes the gap the online tools leave open.

## Warning Signs Beyond the Databases

Some stolen cars slip past a quick lookup, especially cloned ones. Trust these signals too:

- The seller has no title, or the title looks altered.
- The VIN plate is scratched, re-riveted, or freshly glued.
- The price sits far below what the car is worth.
- The seller wants cash only and avoids meeting at a home address.
- The keys look filed down or there is only one, oddly cut key.

Any one of these deserves a second look. Two or more, and the deal is not worth the risk.

## The Bottom Line

The VIN does not announce a stolen car, but it opens every door that does. Confirm the number is genuine, run it through the free databases, and let the police close the gap on anything expensive. A ten-minute check is cheap insurance against losing both the car and the money you paid for it.
